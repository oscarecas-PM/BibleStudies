Preparing for the Resurrection — A Lent Bible Study

PREPARING FOR THE RESURRECTION
A Bible Study on the Origins of Lent

"Those who wait on the Lord shall renew their strength"
Isaiah 40:31

Lent doesn't appear in Scripture by name — it comes from an Old English word for springtime. But the practices it draws on — fasting, waiting, intentional preparation before a defining moment — run deep in the biblical story. This study traces those roots, from the wilderness wanderings of Israel to Jesus' forty days in the desert, and asks what this ancient season might still have to offer.

All Scripture quotations from the NIV. Three parts, meant to be taken slowly.

---

## Part One: The Significance of 40 Days

The number forty appears throughout Scripture as a recurring season of testing, formation, and transition. It's worth tracing the pattern before arriving at Jesus.

### The Wilderness Years

Israel's forty years in the desert were not simply a consequence of disobedience. Deuteronomy reframes them as a deliberate work of formation:

> **Deuteronomy 8:2–3**
> Remember how the Lord your God led you all the way in the wilderness these forty years, to humble and test you in order to know what was in your heart, whether or not you would keep his commands. He humbled you, causing you to hunger and then feeding you with manna… to teach you that man does not live on bread alone but on every word that comes from the mouth of the Lord.

Hunger as teacher. Dependency as the lesson. This passage will appear again.

### Moses on Sinai

Twice Moses spends forty days in God's presence on Sinai. The second time, after the golden calf, is a fast of intercession and renewal:

> **Exodus 34:28**
> Moses was there with the Lord forty days and forty nights without eating bread or drinking water. And he wrote on the tablets the words of the covenant — the Ten Commandments.

When Moses comes down, his face is shining (v. 29–30). Extended nearness to God leaves a mark.

### Jesus in the Desert

Immediately after His baptism — immediately after the Father declares "This is My beloved Son" — Jesus is led into the wilderness. The affirmation of identity comes before the testing.

> **Matthew 4:1–4**
> Then Jesus was led by the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil. After fasting forty days and forty nights, he was hungry… Jesus answered, 'It is written: Man shall not live on bread alone, but on every word that comes from the mouth of God.'

Jesus answers all three temptations with Scripture from Deuteronomy — the same book that describes Israel's forty years. Where Israel failed, He holds. He succeeds not as God overriding human limitation, but as a man sustained by the Word. And He does this before a single miracle, before a single sermon. He prepares.

Lent's forty days (Sundays excluded, as "little Easters") draws on all of this. The tradition is saying: enter the wilderness with Jesus.

### For Discussion

1. Deuteronomy 8 says God "allowed them to hunger" as a form of teaching. How does that reframe the purpose of seasons of spiritual lack or difficulty? Have you experienced that kind of formation?
2. Jesus was "led by the Spirit" into the wilderness — He followed, He didn't choose to go. Lent inverts that: we choose to enter a wilderness of our own making. That's a meaningful difference. Are we entering it to honor God? To prepare ourselves? To face something we've been avoiding? Some would argue that what we fast from comes through the Spirit's prompting, which closes the gap a little — but the choice to observe Lent at all is still ours. Does that change the motivation? And does motivation matter, as long as we're willing to follow where the season leads?
3. Jesus defeated temptation with the very Scripture Israel was given for their wilderness. What does it mean to carry the Word into your own seasons of testing?

## Part Two: What Is a Fast For?

Fasting is one of the oldest spiritual practices in Scripture, and one of the most easily misunderstood. God has something particular to say about what He's looking for.

### The Fast God Chooses

Isaiah 58 is perhaps the sharpest thing in Scripture on this subject. The people are fasting — and frustrated that God seems unmoved. His response is striking:

> **Isaiah 58:3, 6–7**
> 'Why have we fasted, and you have not seen it?'… Is not this the kind of fasting I have chosen: to loose the chains of injustice and untie the cords of the yoke, to set the oppressed free… to share your food with the hungry?

The fast God wants is not self-referential. The hunger you feel is meant to connect you to the hunger of others. What fasting creates in us — awareness of need, dependence, the sensation of emptiness — is supposed to redirect outward.

### Rend Your Heart

> **Joel 2:12–13**
> 'Even now… return to me with all your heart, with fasting and weeping and mourning.' Rend your heart and not your garments. Return to the Lord your God, for he is gracious and compassionate, slow to anger and abounding in love.

The external fast is meaningful only when it corresponds to something internal. "Rend your heart, not your garments" is a rebuke of the gap between form and substance — and an invitation to close it.

### When You Fast

In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus doesn't ask whether His disciples fast — He assumes they will. His concern is the posture:

> **Matthew 6:17–18**
> But when you fast, put oil on your head and wash your face, so that it will not be obvious to others that you are fasting, but only to your Father, who is unseen; and your Father, who sees what is done in secret, will reward you.

The fast that matters is the one no one else knows about. It's between you and God.

A small note on Anna — Luke 2:36–38:

After her husband died, Anna served God with "fastings and prayers night and day." When the infant Jesus was brought to the temple, she recognized Him — while others didn't. The text doesn't explain the connection. It just shows it.

### For Discussion

1. Isaiah 58 describes the fast God chooses in entirely outward, social terms. Is it possible to fast 'correctly' and still be entirely self-focused? What does a fast that opens us to others actually look like?
2. Joel says "rend your heart, not your garments." How do you know when an external practice is connected to genuine internal movement — and when it's just form?
3. What might you fast from this Lent — and what would you hope to notice in its absence?

## Part Three: Waiting on the Lord

Lent's deepest question may be this: what does it mean to prepare yourself to receive something from God? And does the Old Testament reverence surrounding God's presence have anything to say to us about how we approach the resurrection?

### The Weight of the Presence

In the Old Testament, nearness to God is not casual. When the glory fills the tabernacle, Moses cannot enter (Exodus 40:34–35). When the cloud descends on Solomon's temple, the priests cannot stand to minister (1 Kings 8:10–11). Isaiah sees the Lord and his first word is "Woe is me" (Isaiah 6:5).

Preparation wasn't bureaucratic — it was an honest acknowledgment of who God is.

And yet, this same God calls His people to draw near.

### To Wait Is to Seek

The Hebrew word for "wait" — *qavah* — also carries the sense of twisting, binding together, straining toward. It's not passive resignation. It's active hope directed at a specific person.

> **Psalm 27:13–14**
> I remain confident of this: I will see the goodness of the Lord in the land of the living. Wait for the Lord; be strong and take heart and wait for the Lord.

> **Lamentations 3:25–26**
> The Lord is good to those whose hope is in him, to the one who seeks him; it is good to wait quietly for the salvation of the Lord.

Waiting and seeking sit in parallel in Lamentations. To wait on God is to be oriented toward Him — looking, leaning, listening — even in the silence.

### Is Lent an Extension of Old Testament Reverence?

The feasts of the Lord in Leviticus 23 built into the calendar recurring seasons of preparation, fasting, and expectation. Lent draws on the same instinct — but it's oriented differently.

The high priest entered the Most Holy Place through a curtain — with great ceremony, and with fear. When Jesus died, that curtain tore in two (Matthew 27:51). The writer of Hebrews takes that seriously:

> **Hebrews 10:19–22**
> Therefore, brothers and sisters, since we have confidence to enter the Most Holy Place by the blood of Jesus, by a new and living way opened for us through the curtain, that is, his body… let us draw near to God with a sincere heart and with the full assurance that faith brings.

Lent is not a fearful approach to a dangerous God. The way is open. But "draw near with a true heart" is still an invitation that asks something of us. The preparation is not to earn access — it's to arrive at Easter having actually paid attention to what it cost.

The disciples after the resurrection were told to wait (Acts 1:4). They had the commission. They had seen the risen Christ. And still: not yet. Wait for what the Father has promised.

They obeyed. They prayed together for ten days. And then the Spirit fell.

The waiting was the preparation.

### For Discussion

1. The Old Testament treated God's presence as something weighty and even dangerous. Hebrews 10 says we now draw near with boldness. Have we lost something in the transition — or gained something? Both?
2. The disciples were commissioned and then told to wait. Have you ever been in a season of waiting for something God had already promised? What sustained you — or didn't?
3. What would it mean for you to "draw near with a true heart" this Easter? What does your heart need to be true about, coming into this season?

---

Moses' face shone after forty days with God. Paul writes that "we all, who with unveiled faces contemplate the Lord's glory, are being transformed into his image with ever-increasing glory" (2 Corinthians 3:18).

Lent is a season of unveiling — of setting aside enough of the noise and the self-sufficiency that when Easter arrives, we can actually see it. Not as a date. As an event.
